MethodologyThe what-if as a formula, and how the search runs. Open for debate.

The question. f is TabPFN's prediction for one case. S is which inputs may change, v is their new values inside the feasible range FS, and the rest of the case stays as it is. do(XS = v) sets those inputs and reads the model. The search keeps the pair (S*, v*) with the highest score: the highest chance of the class you care about, or a target value or range for a regression.

Pipeline

1 · Set up, once
  • DataRows of inputs x and outcome y.
  • Fit the modelTabPFN-3.5 reads the rows in context. No training run.
  • Feasible rangesAllowed values for each feature.
→
2 · Search loop, many rounds
  • ProposeAn optimization algorithm suggests a batch of input combinations.
  • ScoreOne TabPFN call predicts the whole batch.
  • Learn and repeatThe scores steer the next batch, until a budget of trials is spent.
→
3 · Result
  • Best designThe inputs with the best prediction, shown with its value.
  • Test itThe model's answer, to try in the real setting.
BackgroundWhere the idea comes from, and what it builds on

My brother has diabetes. Every day he asks what insulin dose will keep him in range after a meal. Spielfeld puts that question to a fitted model and searches for an answer. I want to follow it into a paper. The method is open for debate, and I am open to working on it with others.

Pearl · do-operator
Xdo(X = v)Y

Intervene, don't condition. do(X = v) sets a variable instead of observing it. This is the formal language of a what-if. Spielfeld applies it to the inputs of a trained model.

PDP and ICE · one feature
x₁vx₃x₄x₅

One what-if at a time. ICE moves one feature of one case and holds the rest. PDP averages those curves over the data.

Spielfeld · several features
x₁v₂x₃v₄v₅

Many at once, towards a goal. It changes any number of features jointly, inside feasible ranges, and searches for the combination that reaches the goal. No one-feature plot shows such a combination.
